What it is

The Roland Juno-106, released in 1984, is the polyphonic analog synth that taught a generation what "warm" means. Six voices, one digitally controlled oscillator each, a genuine analog low-pass filter, and Roland's lush stereo chorus give it a sound that's instantly recognizable across house, synth-pop, and ambient records. Its layout is famously immediate — a slider or switch for nearly everything — and full MIDI made it the first classic polysynth many producers could actually integrate into a modern setup.

Buying one used — what to check

The Juno-106's signature weakness is its voice chips — the 80017A modules that carry each voice. Their internal resin degrades and shorts over time, producing dropouts, stuck notes, or dead voices; it's the single most important thing to test, so ask the seller to confirm all six voices play cleanly across the keyboard. Reproduction 80017A chips are widely available and a common repair, so a unit with one or two bad voices isn't a dealbreaker if it's priced for it. Beyond the voice chips, check the sliders and the membrane-style buttons, and listen for a healthy chorus — a noisy or silent chorus is another known age issue.

Regional sourcing notes

As a Roland product the Juno-106 is abundant in Japan and among the cleanest-value routes to one; Yahoo Auctions and Mercari list them regularly, often below Western dealer prices. Voltage is the usual catch — domestic units are 100V and want a step-up transformer abroad. In Japanese listings look for ローランド (Roland) and ジュノ or ジュノー (Juno), and read condition terms carefully — 動作確認済み (tested working) and 整備済み (serviced) are reassuring, 美品 means excellent cosmetic condition, and ジャンク (junk) or 未確認 (untested) mean as-is, which for a 106 often signals unaddressed voice-chip problems.
