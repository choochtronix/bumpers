What it is

The Prophet-5, first released by Sequential Circuits in 1978, was the world's first fully programmable polyphonic synthesizer — you could store and recall patches, which changed how synths were played and built. Five voices, two oscillators each, and Sequential's rich, immediate sound made it a defining instrument of its era, heard across countless records. Vintage units come in revisions (Rev 1 and 2 with SSM chips, Rev 3 with CEM chips) that sound subtly different, and Sequential's 2020 reissue brought the design back under founder Dave Smith.

Buying one used — what to check

Revision matters both sonically and practically. The earliest Rev 1 and Rev 2 units use SSM chips and are prized for their tone but are the most temperamental — tuning stability and chip availability are real concerns; Rev 3 units are more stable and more common. Whichever you consider, ask about tuning stability once warm, recent calibration or service, and the state of the potentiometers and switches. Be clear about vintage versus the modern reissue (the 2020 Prophet-5/Prophet-10), which is a superb instrument but not a vintage piece — titles often just say Prophet-5. At these prices, documentation of servicing is worth a lot.

Regional sourcing notes

The Prophet-5 is American-built, so vintage units surface less often in Japan than home-grown Roland or Korg gear — but they do appear on Yahoo Auctions and through Japanese dealers, and the reissue circulates domestically. Voltage runs both ways: a US vintage unit is 117V and a Japanese-market unit is 100V, so confirm which you're buying. In Japanese listings look for シーケンシャル (Sequential) and プロフェット (Prophet); the standard condition vocabulary applies — 動作確認済み (tested working), 整備済み (serviced), 美品 (excellent), against ジャンク (junk) and 未確認 (untested).
